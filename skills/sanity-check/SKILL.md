---
name: sanity-check
description: Use when the user floats an idea, deal, plan, grant, or any claim carrying numbers — before money, time, or a commitment goes in. Stress-tests the math and hunts the fatal constraint first. Also for reviewing someone else's report or pitch.
---
Stress-test the idea the way the user does it himself: find the number that
kills it before admiring the parts that work. Verdict first, always.

1. **Restate the claim as numbers.** Whatever was said in prose — "good
   margin", "big market", "20% growth" — write it as explicit figures with
   units and time ranges. If a figure is missing, mark it MISSING rather than
   assuming one.
2. **Re-derive every number from its raw inputs.** Percentages from both
   endpoints ((new − old) / old — this is where flipped signs and inflated
   growth hide). Totals from their parts. Rates × time for runway and
   deadlines. If your derivation disagrees with the stated number, the stated
   number is wrong until shown otherwise — say so plainly.
3. **Hunt the fatal constraint first.** Every plan has one number or gate most
   likely to kill it: unit economics that never go positive, a market too
   small to matter, a regulatory or eligibility gate, a deadline that can't be
   met, cash that runs out first. Check the most-likely-fatal thing FIRST. If
   it's dead, stop and say it's dead — don't polish a corpse with five more
   pages of analysis.
4. **Steelman, then attack.** State the honest best case in two lines. Then
   list what must be true for it to happen, labeling each assumption `known`
   (with its source) or `guessed`. A plan whose critical assumptions are all
   `guessed` is a guess, not a plan.
5. **Deliver verdict-first, chat-length:**

   **Verdict: kill / proceed / needs one fact** — the single decisive reason.
   - The math (2–4 lines, your derivation, not theirs).
   - What would change the verdict (the one fact to fetch, if any).

   If the full working-out is long, publish it as a page (publish skill) and
   send the verdict + link. Never bury the verdict under the analysis.

Never soften a kill verdict into "worth exploring further" — a clear early no
is the most valuable thing this workflow produces. And never invent a figure
to fill a gap: MISSING is an answer.
